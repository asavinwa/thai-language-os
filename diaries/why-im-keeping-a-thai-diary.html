Why I’m keeping a Thai diary

I’ve realised that writing about what I’m doing can help drive the work itself. I want to try the same thing with Thai.

I don’t need another language-learning plan

I already have songs, stories, listening practice, writing exercises and plenty of opportunities to hear and use Thai in real life. The problem is not finding more material. It is noticing what is actually helping me improve.

So these diaries are going to be a record of the small experiments: trying to stay in Thai for longer, understanding something I would have missed before, getting a phrase wrong, hearing a useful expression repeatedly, or discovering that a way of practising is not helping as much as I expected.

The aim is simple: do something in Thai, notice what happened, keep the useful lesson, then use it to decide what I practise next.

I want progress to come from real life

I’m much more interested in being able to follow a family conversation, understand a joke, use Thai naturally in Thailand or recognise a phrase in a song than in completing a textbook chapter.

That means the useful evidence is going to come from ordinary situations. Where did I switch back to English? What could I understand but not say? Which words kept appearing? What suddenly sounded obvious after months of sounding like noise?

Those are the things I want to capture here.

The diary should feed the learning system

If an entry reveals a phrase I keep needing, it can go into practice. If a pattern keeps appearing in songs, I can build a lesson around it. If listening is still the bottleneck, the next session should respond to that rather than whatever happens to look fun on an app.

I’m not promising a daily diary. I’ll write when something happens that is worth learning from.

Hopefully, over time, this becomes more than a record of studying Thai. It becomes a record of Thai gradually becoming easier to live in.

The rule

Experience before content

Do the Thai first. Write only after there is something real to reflect on.
